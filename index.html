Understanding Testosterone

Testosterone is the main male sex hormone, but its job goes well beyond libido. It helps your body maintain muscle mass, bone density, red blood cell production and fat distribution, and it plays a part in mood, motivation and energy. Men produce most of it in the testes, and the brain controls how much gets made.

Levels usually peak in early adulthood and then fall slowly, often by around 1% a year from middle age onward. That decline is normal. What many men notice is that lifestyle can make it feel steeper.

  1. Sleep
    Most of your daily testosterone is produced while you sleep. Regularly getting less than six or seven hours can lower it noticeably within days.
  2. Body weight
    Extra body fat, especially around the waist, is linked with lower testosterone. Even modest weight loss often helps.
  3. Exercise
    Strength training and staying active support healthy levels. Very long, intense endurance training with too little recovery can have the opposite effect.
  4. Stress and alcohol
    Long-term stress raises cortisol, which works against testosterone. Heavy drinking can also reduce it.
  5. Nutrition
    Zinc, vitamin D, magnesium and enough overall calories and healthy fats all matter. A supplement can help cover gaps, but it can't replace a balanced diet.

A note on when to see a doctor: if you have persistent fatigue, a clear drop in sex drive, difficulty with erections, loss of muscle or ongoing low mood, ask your doctor about a morning blood test. Those symptoms can have many causes, and true low testosterone is a medical condition that a supplement isn't meant to treat.
